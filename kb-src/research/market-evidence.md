## 1. Problem statistics (detail)

### 1.1 Polypharmacy prevalence (5+ medicines) in adults 65+
- **NCHS / NHANES (official):** adults 65+ using 5+ prescription drugs in the past 30 days rose from **33.3% (2001 to 2004) to 43.0% (2017 to March 2020)**. Use of 3+ drugs rose from 59.8% to **67.7%**. Civilian non-institutionalized population. Prescription only; OTC drugs and supplements are excluded. [P] https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/hus/topics/rx-drug-use.htm
- **Wang et al., Glob Health Res Policy 2023 (NHANES 1999 to 2018):** polypharmacy among older adults rose from **23.5% to 44.1%**. Among adults with heart disease it was 61.7% in 2017-2018, and with diabetes 57.7%. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37434230/
- **Kantor et al., JAMA 2015:** among all US adults, polypharmacy rose from 8.2% (1999-2000) to 15% (2011-2012). [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26529160/
- **Qato et al., JAMA Intern Med 2016 (in-home medication inspection, ages 62 to 85):** concurrent use of 5+ prescription medicines was **35.8%** in 2010-2011. Dietary supplement use was **63.7%**. **15.1%** were at risk of a potential major drug-drug interaction, up from 8.4% in 2005-2006. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26998708/
  - Product implication: the medication list must capture OTC drugs and supplements. The NCHS figure excludes them, so true medicine counts are higher.

### 1.2 Adverse drug events (ADEs): ED visits and hospitalizations in older adults
- **Budnitz et al., NEJM 2011 (2007 to 2009):** an estimated **99,628 emergency hospitalizations per year** for ADEs in adults 65+. **48.1%** were in people 80+, and **65.7%** were unintentional overdoses. Four drug groups were implicated in **67.0%**: warfarin 33.3%, insulins 13.9%, oral antiplatelets 13.3%, oral hypoglycemics 10.7%. "High-risk" (Beers-type) medicines were implicated in only **1.2%**. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22111719/
- **Shehab et al., JAMA 2016 (2013 to 2014):** 4.0 ADE ED visits per 1,000 people per year across all ages. Adults 65+ accounted for **34.5%** of these visits (25.6% in 2005-2006) and had the highest hospitalization rate (**43.6%**). In older adults, anticoagulants, diabetes agents and opioid analgesics were implicated in **59.9%** of visits. Beers "always avoid" medicines were implicated in **1.8%**. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27893129/
- **Budnitz et al., JAMA 2021 (2017 to 2019, 60 EDs, 96,925 cases):** **12.1 vs 5.0** ED visits per 1,000 for 65+ vs under-65. **95.8%** of older-adult visits involved therapeutic use. The most common causes in 65+ were therapeutic anticoagulant use (4.5 per 1,000) and diabetes agents (1.8 per 1,000). Warfarin was implicated in **20.7%** of visits in 65+. Hospitalization followed **48.9%** of visits in people 75+. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34609453/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8493432/
- **Cost:** non-optimized medication therapy cost an estimated **US$528.4 billion in 2016** (plausible range $495.3B to $672.7B), about **16%** of US health spending, all ages. [P] Watanabe et al., Ann Pharmacother 2018 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29577766/
- **Advocacy framing (cite as a think-tank estimate, not a peer-reviewed figure):** Lown Institute, "Medication Overload": "every day, 750 older people ... are hospitalized due to serious side effects". [A] https://lowninstitute.org/projects/medication-overload-how-the-drive-to-prescribe-is-harming-older-americans/

> **Rigor point for judges (important):** Beers "avoid" drugs drive only a small share of acute ADE visits (1.2% to 1.8%). Most acute harm comes from anticoagulants, insulin and other diabetes agents, antiplatelets and opioids, which are often appropriate drugs that need monitoring. MedWatch should therefore (a) run Beers, anticholinergic and fall-risk checks for chronic or cumulative harms (falls, cognition), and (b) add a "monitor-for" symptom watch-list for these high-ADE classes: bleeding and bruising or black stools on anticoagulants, shakiness, sweating and confusion (possible hypoglycemia) on insulin or sulfonylureas, and sedation and constipation on opioids. Saying this in the pitch shows we read the evidence instead of over-claiming Beers.

### 1.3 Falls, and the share linked to medicines (FRIDs)
- **CDC (updated Sept 2026):** more than **1 in 4** older people fall each year, but **less than half tell their doctor**. Falling once doubles the chance of falling again. About **4.5 million** ED visits per year (3.1 million treated and released, **1.4 million hospitalizations**). Nearly **319,000** hip-fracture hospitalizations per year. The CDC lists "use of medicines, such as tranquilizers, sedatives, or antidepressants" (and some OTC medicines) as a **modifiable** risk factor. [P] https://www.cdc.gov/falls/data-research/facts-stats/index.html
- **CDC:** falls among adults 65+ caused **over 43,000 deaths in 2024**, making them the leading cause of injury death in that group. [P] https://www.cdc.gov/falls/about/index.html
- **Cost:** **US$80.0 billion** in 2020 for non-fatal falls (Haddad et al., Inj Prev 2024, CDC authors), up from about $50 billion in 2015. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39029927/
- **FRID exposure trend (Shaver et al., Pharmacoepidemiol Drug Saf 2021; MEPS + NVSS, 1999 to 2017):** the share of persons 65+ receiving at least one fall-risk-increasing drug (STEADI-Rx list) rose from **57% to 94%**. Age-adjusted fall mortality rose from 29.40 to 63.27 per 100,000. Antidepressant recipients rose from 7% to 16% of the 65+ population. Caveat: the STEADI-Rx list includes antihypertensives, and the study shows an ecological trend, not causation. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33534172/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8254780/
- **Effect sizes (EuGMS FRID meta-analyses, adjusted pooled ORs, ages 60+):**
  - Psychotropics: antipsychotics 1.54, antidepressants 1.57, SSRIs 2.02, TCAs 1.41, benzodiazepines 1.42 (long-acting 1.81). [P] Seppala et al., JAMDA 2018 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29402652/
  - Others: opioids 1.60, antiepileptics 1.55, polypharmacy 1.75. NSAIDs (1.09) and analgesics overall were not significant. [P] Seppala et al., JAMDA 2018 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29402646/
  - Cardiovascular: loop diuretics 1.36. Beta-blockers 0.88 (lower risk), so do **not** flag all antihypertensives equally. [P] de Vries et al., JAMDA 2018 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29396189/
- **Share of falls attributable to medicines:** **UNVERIFIED**. We did not find a reliable US population-attributable fraction. Do not state "X% of falls are caused by medications". Use "medications are one of the most modifiable fall risk factors (CDC)" plus the ORs above.

### 1.4 Anticholinergic burden and dementia risk
- **Coupland et al., JAMA Intern Med 2019 (nested case-control, QResearch England, 58,769 dementia cases, 225,574 controls, ages 55+):** the adjusted OR for dementia rose from 1.06 (1 to 90 TSDDs) to **1.49 (>1,095 TSDDs)**. ORs were elevated for anticholinergic antidepressants (1.29), antiparkinson drugs (1.52), antipsychotics (1.70), bladder antimuscarinics (1.65) and antiepileptics (1.39). The population-attributable fraction was **10.3%**. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31233095/
- **Gray et al., JAMA Intern Med 2015 (ACT cohort, Seattle, 3,434 people 65+):** HR **1.54** for dementia with cumulative use of more than 1,095 TSDDs over 10 years, with a dose-response trend. The most common classes were TCAs, first-generation antihistamines and bladder antimuscarinics. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25621434/
- **Trend + mortality (Age Ageing 2026, NHANES 1988 to March 2020, 16,188 older adults):** the prevalence of high anticholinergic burden (score >= 3) **declined**, but high burden remained associated with all-cause mortality (ACB scale HR 1.54). [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41785066/
- Wording: observational associations. Say "associated with", never "causes dementia".

### 1.5 Potentially inappropriate medication (PIM) use per Beers in US older adults
- **AGS 2023 Beers Criteria** (JAGS 2023;71:2052-2081): for adults 65+ in all settings except hospice and end-of-life care. They are designed for the US and "should be applied thoughtfully ... in a manner that supports, rather than replaces, shared clinical decision-making". Use this framing in the product. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37139824/
- **Davidoff et al., JAGS 2015 (MEPS 2006 to 2010, community-dwelling, n = 18,475, 2012 Beers):** **42.6%** of older adults with prescriptions had at least one PIM fill (broad definition), or **30.9%** using a qualified definition. NSAIDs were the most common PIM at 10.9%. Use declined from 45.5% to 40.8%. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25752646/
- **Tian et al., JAMA Netw Open 2023 (meta-analysis, outpatients):** pooled global PIM prevalence **36.7%**, North America **29.0%**. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37531105/
- **Ndai et al., JAGS 2024 (Medicare FFS 2018):** **88%** of beneficiaries were dispensed at least one PIM across 8 PIM tools combined (broad union definition, so not a Beers-only number). **50%** of index-drug classes and **47%** of marker-drug classes in known prescribing cascades are PIMs. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38124261/
- **Dementia subgroup (Medicare 2011 to 2017):** **32.7%** used one or more PIMs per year (anticholinergics 14.9%, benzodiazepines 14.0%, antipsychotics 11.0%). [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37038818/
- Safe single line: "Roughly 3 to 4 in 10 older adults use at least one potentially inappropriate medication (Davidoff 2015; Tian 2023)."

### 1.6 Prescribing cascades
- **Definition and tool:** ThinkCascades (McCarthy et al., Drugs Aging 2022) is a Delphi consensus of 38 clinicians from 6 countries that produced **9 clinically important cascades**. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36107399/
- **Prevalence (Doherty et al., Ann Fam Med 2025; Ireland, 533,464 adults 65+, 2017 to 2020):** 5 of the 9 ThinkCascades showed significant signals:
  - CCB to diuretic: prevalence **2.6%**, aSR 1.93
  - alpha-1 blocker to vestibular sedative: **3.0%**
  - SSRI/SNRI to sleep medicine: **2.5%**
  - benzodiazepine to antipsychotic: **3.2%**
  - antipsychotic to antiparkinsonian: **0.4%**

  Note: these are non-US data. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40494622/
- **Canonical example for the demo (Savage et al., JAMA Intern Med 2020; Ontario, 41,086 older adults newly started on a CCB):** 90-day incidence of a new loop diuretic was **1.4% vs 0.7%/0.5%** in the control groups. The hazard of a loop-diuretic start rose to **2.40** (vs ACEi/ARB) and **3.89** (vs unrelated drugs) in days 61 to 90. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32091538/
- **US dementia population (Singh et al., PDS 2021):** 4.3% of prevalent CCB users with ADRD started a diuretic, which the authors called "low". [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33715299/
- **Demo story grounded in evidence:** amlodipine started, ankle swelling logged 3 weeks later, then a "water pill" added. MedWatch shows the timeline and the cascade pattern as a question for the clinician, not a conclusion.
- Safe wording: "Individual cascades affect roughly 1 to 3% of older adults each. They are individually uncommon but collectively important, and hard to see without a timeline."

### 1.7 Family caregivers (AARP/NAC, Caregiving in the US 2025; survey of 6,858 caregivers, released July 2025)
Source for all bullets: [P] report PDF https://www.aarp.org/content/dam/aarp/ppi/topics/ltss/family-caregiving/caregiving-in-us-2025.doi.10.26419-2fppi.00373.001.pdf ; landing page https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/ltss/family-caregiving/caregiving-in-the-us-2025/ ; press release https://www.aarp.org/press/releases/2025-07-24-new-report-reveals-crisis-point-for-americas-63-million-family-caregivers.html
- **63 million** Americans are family caregivers, about 1 in 4 adults. That is a 45 to 50% increase since 2015; AARP pages use both "nearly 50%" and "45%".
- **55%** help with **"giving medicines, pills, or injections"** (IADL item, up from 50% in 2015).
- **55%** perform medical/nursing tasks (defined to include giving medicines, monitoring BP or blood sugar, and wound care). Only **22%** received training for these tasks, and **22%** say the tasks are difficult.
- **70%** monitor the severity of the recipient's condition, **64%** communicate with health professionals, and **58%** advocate with providers.
- **48%** care for someone aged **75+**. The average care-recipient age is **69.8**. The average caregiver age is **51**.
- Product implication: the primary user is a 51-year-old adult child (smartphone- and web-literate) acting for a parent in their late 70s. The older adult contributes symptoms in their own words.

### 1.8 Visit length and time for medication review
- **Neprash et al., Med Care 2021 (21.0 million primary-care visits, 2017 EHR timestamps):** mean exam **18.0 minutes**; exams ran 1.2 minutes over schedule on average. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33301282/
- **Tai-Seale et al., Health Serv Res 2007 (392 videotaped visits of elderly patients):** median visit **15.7 minutes** covering a median of **6 topics**. The longest topic got about 5 minutes; the others got about **1.1 minutes** each. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17850524/
- **Porter et al., JGIM 2023:** a PCP would need **26.7 hours/day** to deliver all guideline-recommended care to a 2,500-patient panel (9.3 h/day with team-based care). [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35776372/
- **Tarn et al., Arch Intern Med 2006:** when prescribing a new medicine, physicians addressed adverse effects for only **35%** of new prescriptions. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17000942/
- **Specific minutes spent on medication review within a primary-care visit: UNVERIFIED.** We found no strong US source. Do not quote a number. Use the "1.1 minutes per secondary topic" finding instead.

### 1.9 Under-reporting and recall of symptoms and side effects
- **Gandhi et al., NEJM 2003 (4 Boston primary-care practices, 661 survey respondents):** **25%** had an ADE (27 per 100 patients). 13% of events were serious, 28% ameliorable and 11% preventable. Of the ameliorable events, **63%** came from the physician failing to respond to medication-related symptoms and **37%** from the patient failing to tell the physician. (The app and README quote the counts behind these percentages: 19 and 32 of 51 ameliorable events. They match the percentages here, 28% of the 181 events being 51, but were not re-checked against the abstract when this note was added on 2026-10-17; confirm at the PubMed link before quoting the counts in new material.) The number of medications was the only significant predictor. The authors conclude that "monitoring for and acting on symptoms are important". [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12700376/
- **CDC:** less than half of older adults who fall tell their doctor. [P] https://www.cdc.gov/falls/data-research/facts-stats/index.html
- **Basch et al., JNCI 2009 (lung cancer):** patients reported symptoms **earlier and more frequently** than clinicians, and patient reports tracked daily health status better. The authors call the two perspectives complementary. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19920223/ ; commentary: Basch, "The missing voice of patients in drug-safety reporting", NEJM 2010 https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20220181/
- **Kessels, J R Soc Med 2003 (review):** "40-80% of medical information provided by healthcare practitioners is forgotten" immediately. This supports a written, structured brief rather than memory. [P] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC539473/

### 1.10 Medication discrepancies (outpatient and transitions)
- **Bedell et al., Arch Intern Med 2000 (312 outpatients, bottle check vs chart):** **76%** had at least one discrepancy. 51% were drugs taken but not recorded, 29% recorded but not taken, and 20% dose differences. Older age and number of medicines were the strongest predictors. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10904455/
- **Tam et al., CMAJ 2005 (systematic review, 22 studies):** medication-history errors at admission occurred in up to **67%** of cases. 11 to 59% of these errors were clinically important. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16129874/
- **Cornish et al., Arch Intern Med 2005:** **53.6%** of admitted patients had at least one unintended discrepancy. The most common was omission (46.4%), and **38.6%** of discrepancies could cause moderate to severe harm. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15738372/
- **Coleman et al., Arch Intern Med 2005 (adults 65+ after discharge):** **14.1%** had post-discharge discrepancies, and those patients had higher 30-day readmission (**14.3% vs 6.1%**). [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16157827/
- Product implication: a caregiver-maintained list that includes OTC drugs and supplements, with start and stop dates, is valuable on its own. The brief should show the list "as actually taken".

### 1.11 Value of symptom diaries / patient-reported outcomes (PROs)
- **Basch et al., JCO 2016 (RCT, 766 patients on chemotherapy):** symptom self-reporting improved quality of life (HRQL improved in 34% vs 18%) and reduced ER visits (34% vs 41%). 1-year survival was 75% vs 69%. Benefits were larger for patients **lacking prior computer experience**. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26644527/
- **Basch et al., JAMA 2017 (same trial):** median overall survival **31.2 vs 26.0 months** (P = .03). [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28586821/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5817466/
- **Yang et al., Support Care Cancer 2018 (systematic review, 43 studies):** PROs facilitate patient-clinician communication by increasing symptom awareness, prompting discussion and streamlining consultations. Providing **easily accessible data summaries** for clinicians is a facilitator. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28849277/
- **Meirte et al., JMIR Perioper Med 2020 (systematic review, 32 studies of ePROMs):** electronic collection improves data quality and response rates and facilitates symptom management and communication. Privacy and the digital divide are disadvantages. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33393920/
- **Honest caveat:** the strongest PRO outcome evidence is in oncology. Present it as "evidence that structured symptom reporting changes care", not as proof that MedWatch improves geriatric outcomes.

### 1.12 Demand side: openness to deprescribing
- **Reeve et al., JAMA Intern Med 2018 (NHATS, n = 1,981 Medicare beneficiaries 65+):** **92.0%** would stop one or more medicines if their physician said it was possible, and **66.6%** want fewer medicines. People on 6+ medicines had 2.90 times the odds of willingness. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30326004/

### 1.13 Digital readiness and privacy context
- **Pew Research Center (survey Feb to June 2025):** **78%** of adults 65+ own a smartphone, and 95% own a cellphone. [P] https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/
- **Grundy et al., BMJ 2019:** **79%** of 24 medicines-related Android apps shared user data with 55 entities. 67% of those entities provided analytics or advertising services. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30894349/
- **Grundy et al., JGIM 2019:** **71%** (17/24) of medicines-related apps described at least one promotional or commercial use of user data. [P] https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31529374/
- These support the local-first design. No patient-data server means no data-sharing ecosystem to audit.

### 1.14 Regulatory positioning (useful for "real-world applicability"; not legal advice)
- **FDA, Policy for Device Software Functions and Mobile Medical Applications (Sept 2022):**
  - **Appendix A (not medical devices)** includes software giving patients "simple tools to organize and record their health information" without recommending changes to prescribed therapy (#12), and software "specifically marketed to help patients document, show, or communicate to health care professionals regarding potential medical conditions" (#13).
  - **Appendix B (enforcement discretion)** #18 covers software that gives "patients and/or caregivers information about drug-drug interactions and relevant safety information ... as a report based on demographic data ..., clinical information ..., and current medications".

  [P] https://www.fda.gov/media/80958/download ; landing page https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/policy-device-software-functions-and-mobile-medical-applications
- **FDA Clinical Decision Support Software guidance (final, January 2026; supersedes 2022):** the non-device CDS criteria for HCP-facing software include letting the professional **independently review the basis** of recommendations. This is why each flag in the clinician brief should cite its criterion (e.g., "AGS Beers 2023, Table 2") and show the inputs. **What the landscape tells us (for the pitch):**
1. Reminders, med lists and drug-drug interaction checks are commodities (Apple, Medisafe, Drugs.com, pharmacy apps). Do **not** pitch MedWatch as "an interaction checker".
2. Symptom diaries exist (MyTherapy, CareClinic, Apple Symptoms). Do **not** claim to be the first symptom tracker.
3. Geriatric-specific screening (Beers/STOPP, deprescribing) lives in **clinician tools** (MedStopper, MedSafer, deprescribing.org, MedWise). These rely on the clinician to supply the list and do not use what the family observes at home.
4. General LLM health assistants (ChatGPT Health) are now mainstream. They are cloud-based and non-deterministic, and no geriatric criteria engine is documented.
5. MedSafer's RCT is a warning: rule-based flags alone increased deprescribing **without** reducing short-term ADEs. MedWatch's added value has to come from patient-observed symptoms and timing, which the clinician tools lack. The honest outcome claim is better-informed conversations, not fewer ADEs (yet).

**Differentiation statement (honest, no "first ever"):**
> MedWatch is not another reminder app or interaction checker. It brings together three things that the consumer tools we reviewed do not document doing together. First, it screens the person's own medication list against geriatric criteria clinicians already use: AGS Beers 2023, anticholinergic burden, fall-risk-increasing drugs and known prescribing cascades. Second, it keeps a symptom timeline captured in the older adult's own words and lines it up with medication start and stop dates. It shows counts and denominators and makes no causal claims. Third, it produces a one-page, source-cited brief built for a 15-minute visit or a pharmacist's medication review. All of this runs in the browser, so there is no patient-data server to breach or monetize. Clinician tools have the criteria but not the home observations. Diary apps have the observations but not the criteria. MedWatch connects the two for the visit.

**Claims to avoid:** "first", "only", "detects side effects", "prevents falls/ADEs", "clinically validated", "FDA approved/exempt", "HIPAA-compliant" (HIPAA generally does not apply to a consumer tool with no covered-entity relationship; say "no server, no account, data stays on this device"), "diagnoses".

## 3. Recent literature (2024 to 2026): LLMs for medication review, PGHD summarization, on-device LLMs

Core set (cite 4 to 8 of these):

1. **Socrates V, Wright DS, et al. "Identifying Deprescribing Opportunities With Large Language Models in Older Adults." JMIR Aging 2025;8:e69504.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40215480/
   - The LLM pipeline was good at **finding relevant criteria** (Beers/STOPP/GEMS-Rx; PPV 0.83, NPV 0.93) but weak at **deciding whether a criterion applies** (PPV 0.47). 54.5% of errors came from complex inclusion/exclusion rules and 39.3% from missing context. Confidence was poorly calibrated.
   - **MedWatch lesson:** keep criteria matching deterministic and leave applicability to the clinician.
2. **Ong JCL, et al. "Large language model as clinical decision support system augments medication safety in 16 clinical specialties." Cell Rep Med 2025;6(10):102323.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40997804/
   - A RAG-grounded LLM paired with a pharmacist ("co-pilot") did best: 61% accuracy, and 1.5 times the pharmacist-alone accuracy for serious-harm errors.
   - **Lesson:** grounding plus a human in the loop, not autonomy.
3. **Gallifant J, et al. "Reliability of Large Language Model Knowledge Across Brand and Generic Cancer Drug Names." JCO Clin Cancer Inform 2025;9:e2400257.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40523223/
   - GPT-3.5/4/4o matched brand and generic names well (about 95 to 97%), but **DDI detection accuracy was below 26%** on 1,000 synthetic cases.
   - **Lesson:** never let a generative model decide interactions. Use curated rule tables and let the LLM handle language.
4. **Blotske K, et al. "Drug-drug interaction identification using large language models." medRxiv 2025 (published J Manag Care Spec Pharm 2026;32(9):1076-1089).** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41503479/
   - Tested GPT-4o-mini, MedGemma-27B and LLaMA3-70B on 750 clinician-annotated DDI scenarios. Performance and self-consistency **degraded as the number of drugs grew (4 to 6 drug lists)**, which is exactly the polypharmacy case.
5. **Van Veen D, et al. "Adapted large language models can outperform medical experts in clinical text summarization." Nat Med 2024;30:1134-1142.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38413730/
   - In a 10-physician reader study, the best adapted LLM summaries were equivalent (45%) or superior (36%) to expert summaries. Fabrication types were documented.
   - **Lesson:** summarizing a free-text diary is a reasonable LLM job if each claim can be traced back to its source.
6. **Asgari E, et al. "A framework to assess clinical safety and hallucination rates of LLMs for medical text summarisation." npj Digit Med 2025;8:274.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40360677/
   - Across 12,999 clinician-annotated sentences: **1.47% hallucination** and **3.45% omission** rates. Prompt and workflow iteration reduced major errors.
   - **Lesson:** show the user the source sentence for every extracted symptom and require review before anything enters the brief.
7. **Wiest IC, et al. "Privacy-preserving large language models for structured medical information retrieval." npj Digit Med 2024;7:257.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39304709/
   - A **local** Llama-2 pipeline extracted features from 500 MIMIC-IV histories. Symptom extraction worked (e.g., confusion: sensitivity 76%, specificity 94%), and the 70B model beat smaller versions.
   - **Lesson:** local extraction is feasible, but small models are weaker. Constrain the 1.5B in-browser model to schema-bound extraction with a rule-based fallback.
8. **Ruan CF, et al. "WebLLM: A High-Performance In-Browser LLM Inference Engine." arXiv:2412.15803 (2024).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15803
   - In-browser inference via WebGPU/WebAssembly retains **up to 80% of native performance**. The authors frame it as enabling "privacy-preserving, personalized, and locally powered" applications. This is the engine MedWatch uses.

Supplementary (optional):
- **Fujita K, et al. Drug Saf 2026 (online Sept 2026).** A two-stage **rule-based extraction + LLM classification** system found deprescribing recommendations in 850 discharge summaries (F1 0.91), explicitly for "privacy-compliant local deployment". This is architecturally the same pattern as MedWatch. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42690283/
- **Rao A, et al. J Med Syst 2024.** ChatGPT-3.5 on deprescribing vignettes recommended deprescribing almost universally in patients without CVD and gave inconsistent answers across replicates. This is evidence against free-form LLM deprescribing advice. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38632172/
- **PH-LLM (Google), Nat Med 2025;31:3394-3403.** An LLM fine-tuned to reason over daily wearable data (patient-generated health data) for sleep and fitness performed similarly to experts on fitness tasks. Shows interest in LLM summarization of PGHD; it is cloud-based and not geriatric. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40813712/

**One-paragraph "state of the art and its risks" for the PDF or video:**
> Recent studies show LLMs are useful for reading and summarizing clinical language (Van Veen 2024). They still hallucinate or omit details at measurable rates (Asgari 2025), and they are unreliable at the drug-safety judgments themselves. One evaluation put drug-drug interaction detection below 26% accuracy (Gallifant 2025), another found performance fell as medication lists grew (Blotske 2026), and an LLM deprescribing pipeline found the right criteria but often misapplied them (Socrates 2025). The best results come from grounded models working alongside a pharmacist (Ong 2025). MedWatch follows that evidence. A small on-device model handles only language (turning "I felt woozy after lunch" into a structured, reviewable symptom entry). Every medication flag comes from a deterministic, citation-backed rule table. A person reviews everything before it reaches the clinician.

## 4. Problem statements and taglines (each grounded in a cited statistic)

**Problem statements (one line each):**
1. "43% of Americans over 65 take five or more prescription drugs (NCHS), yet a primary-care visit lasts about 18 minutes and secondary topics get about a minute each (Neprash 2021; Tai-Seale 2007), which is not enough time to connect a new symptom to last month's new pill."
2. "In primary care, a quarter of patients have an adverse drug event, and over a third of the fixable ones persisted because the patient never told the doctor about the symptom (Gandhi, NEJM 2003)."
3. "More than 1 in 4 older adults fall each year and fewer than half tell their doctor (CDC), while 55% of America's 63 million family caregivers already give medicines with almost no training (AARP/NAC 2025)."

**Taglines:**
1. **"Five medicines. Fifteen minutes. One page."** Grounded in: 43% of adults 65+ on 5+ Rx (NCHS); median visit 15.7 min (Tai-Seale 2007) and mean 18.0 min (Neprash 2021).
2. **"Noticed at home. Discussed at the visit."** Grounded in: fewer than half of older adults who fall tell their doctor (CDC); 37% of ameliorable outpatient ADEs went unreported by patients (Gandhi 2003).
3. **"Checked against the evidence. Kept on your device."** Grounded in: AGS Beers 2023 criteria; 79% of medicines-related apps shared user data (Grundy, BMJ 2019).
